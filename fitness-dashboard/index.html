    Metabolic priming

    Set up your body to burn, perform and recover

    "Metabolic priming" covers the habits that prepare your metabolism for a day, a workout, or a diet phase: light timing, fueling, movement between meals, and how you cycle calories. Some have strong evidence. Others are marketed far beyond what the data show. Each strategy below carries an evidence grade and the realistic size of its effect.

    A primed day

    Times assume a 6:30 wake-up and an evening workout. Shift the whole schedule to fit yours.

    Metabolic adaptation, in numbers

    Adaptive thermogenesis
    Energy use drops about 5–15% beyond what weight loss alone predicts, mostly via reduced NEAT and hormonal shifts (leptin, thyroid T3).
    Muscle at rest
    About 13 kcal per kg per day, versus about 4.5 for fat. Adding 3 kg of muscle raises BMR by roughly 40 kcal. Lifting matters more for what it does to body composition and insulin sensitivity.
    Sleep restriction
    On 5.5 h versus 8.5 h, dieters lost the same weight but 55% less of it came from fat (Nedeltcheva 2010).
    Diet breaks
    MATADOR trial: 2 weeks dieting, 2 weeks at maintenance, repeated. More fat lost and less rebound than continuous dieting.
    Fasted cardio
    No extra fat loss when daily calories are matched (Schoenfeld 2014).

    Adaptation check

    Weeks in a deficit and signs you notice suggest whether a diet break is due.

    Meal prep system

    • Batch 2× a week (e.g. Sunday and Wednesday): cooked proteins keep 3–4 days refrigerated.
    • Cook components, not meals: 2 proteins, 2 carbs, 2 roasted vegetables, 2 sauces mix into many meals.
    • Weigh cooked batches: weigh the full pot of rice, divide by portions, and log the cooked weight.
    • Freeze extras: chili, curries, burritos and meatballs freeze 2–3 months.
    • Default breakfasts: one or two automatic breakfasts remove a daily decision.
    • Snack kit: Greek yogurt, jerky, protein bars (20 g+ protein, under 250 kcal), fruit, cottage cheese.
    • Eating out: pick a protein plus vegetables, ask for sauce on the side, and estimate about 1.5× the menu calories for restaurant dishes.
    • Food safety: cool food within 2 h, reheat to 74 °C / 165 °F, keep the fridge at 4 °C / 40 °F or below.

    Reading a nutrition label

    • Check the serving size first; packages often hold 2–4 servings.
    • Protein per 100 kcal: 10 g or more is a high-protein food.
    • Fiber: 3 g or more per serving is a good source.
    • Added sugar: keep under 10% of calories (about 50 g on 2,000 kcal).
    • Sodium: 20% DV or more is high. Aim under 2,300 mg a day unless you sweat heavily.
    • Ingredients are listed by weight. A long list of additives suggests ultra-processed food.
    • US labels may round anything under 0.5 g down to 0 (trans fat, sugar in sprays).

    Ultra-processed food

    The NOVA system groups foods: 1) unprocessed (fruit, meat, eggs), 2) culinary ingredients (oil, butter, salt), 3) processed (cheese, canned fish, fresh bread), 4) ultra-processed (soft drinks, chips, packaged snacks, most cereals).

    In a tightly controlled NIH trial (Hall 2019), people ate about 500 kcal more per day on an ultra-processed diet than on an unprocessed one, even though both diets offered the same nutrients, and gained weight.

    Many processed foods are useful (whey, canned beans, frozen vegetables, Greek yogurt). Aim for roughly 80% minimally processed.

    Fad diet tracker

    What the trend promises versus what it delivers

    Nearly every fad diet works the same way: it cuts calories by banning foods, and the scale drops (often water and glycogen first). The verdict reflects safety, sustainability and whether the claimed mechanism holds up.

    Red-flag check

    Thinking about a new diet or program? Tick every claim it makes.

    Why the first week always "works"

    Glycogen
    Each gram of stored carbohydrate holds about 3 g of water. Cutting carbs can drop 1–3 kg in days with no fat lost.
    Gut content
    Eating less food volume means less weight in the digestive tract.
    Sodium
    Cutting processed food lowers sodium and water retention.
    Novelty
    Strict rules cut calories at first. Adherence typically fades after 4–12 weeks.
    Real fat loss
    A 500 kcal/day deficit loses about 0.45 kg (1 lb) of fat per week. Anything much faster is mostly water.

    Progressive overload methods

    • Double progression: hit the top of the rep range on all sets, then add 2.5–5% load.
    • Add a set: grow weekly volume within a training block.
    • Reduce rest at the same load (conditioning focus).
    • Slow the tempo: 3-second eccentrics add time under tension.

    • Frequency: train a muscle 2× instead of 1× per week.

    Periodization models

    Linear
    Gradually add load while reps fall across weeks (e.g. 3×12 → 3×10 → 3×8 → 3×5). Best for beginners and peaking.
    Daily undulating (DUP)
    Vary the rep range within the week: heavy (3 to 5), moderate (6 to 10), light (12 to 15). Often better for intermediates.
    Block
    Accumulation (volume), transmutation (intensity), then realization (peak), each 2 to 4 weeks. Common in sport.
    Conjugate
    Rotating maximal-effort and dynamic-effort days with frequent exercise changes (Westside style). Needs experience.
    Autoregulated
    Loads set by RPE or reps in reserve, so heavy days adapt to how you feel.
    Deload
    Every 4 to 8 weeks cut volume 40 to 60% for a week.

    Progressive overload, step by step

    • Pick a rep range per lift (e.g. 6 to 10).
    • Start with a load you can lift for the bottom of the range with 2 reps in reserve.
    • Add a rep each session until you hit the top on all sets.
    • Then add the smallest load jump (2.5 kg / 5 lb, or 1 to 2 kg on small muscles) and restart at the bottom.
    • Stalled two sessions in a row? Check sleep and calories, drop 10%, and rebuild.
    • Log every set on the Programs tab; it suggests the next load for you.

    Heat, altitude, cold & travel

    Heat
    Endurance performance falls noticeably above about 20 °C / 68 °F. Heat acclimation takes 10–14 days of 60–90 min sessions in the heat. Pre-cool with cold drinks or slushies, and drink to thirst plus electrolytes. Stop for headache, confusion or chills; heat stroke is an emergency.
    Altitude
    Above about 1,500 m, VO₂max drops roughly 6–7% per 1,000 m. Allow 1–2 weeks to adapt, cut intensity at first, raise fluids, and check iron status before a training camp.
    Cold
    Warm up longer, dress in layers (wicking base, insulating middle, windproof shell), protect hands and ears. Cold air can trigger exercise-induced asthma.
    Air quality
    Move hard sessions indoors when AQI exceeds about 150, or 100 if you have asthma or heart disease.
    Jet lag
    Adjusting takes about 1 day per time zone. Get morning light after flying east and evening light after flying west. Train outdoors in daylight at the destination; keep sessions easy for 1–2 days.
    Concurrent training: cardio barely blunts muscle gain when you separate hard cardio from leg sessions by 6+ hours (or put lifting first), favor cycling or incline walking over high-volume running, and eat enough.

    Cycling power zones (FTP)

    FTP is roughly the highest power you can hold for about an hour. A common test is 95% of your best 20-minute power. Zones follow the Coggan model.

    Swim pace (critical swim speed)

    Swim both distances all-out with a full rest between. CSS pace per 100 m = (T400 − T200) ÷ 2.

    Zone 2 and base building

    Talk test
    You can speak in full sentences but not sing. Nose breathing is comfortable.
    Heart rate
    About 60 to 70% of heart-rate reserve (see your zones above). The "180 − age" MAF rule gives a rough ceiling of bpm.
    Dose
    2 to 4 hours a week in sessions of 45 to 90 minutes. Build by about 10% a week.
    Why
    It builds mitochondria and capillaries, improves fat use and recovery between hard days.
    Pair it with
    One or two hard interval sessions a week (4×4 min or 30/30s) for VO₂max, and strength training twice weekly.
    Skip when
    HRV is well below baseline, resting heart rate is up 5+ bpm, or you're ill.

    Recovery

    Readiness, sleep & deloads

    Adaptation happens between sessions. Check in each morning to decide whether to push, train as planned, or pull back.

    Morning readiness check-in

    Sleep checklist

    • 7–9 h in bed-to-wake window, same wake time daily (±30 min)
    • Bright light in the morning, dim light 1–2 h before bed
    • Bedroom around 18 °C / 65 °F, dark and quiet
    • Caffeine cutoff 8–10 h before bed
    • Alcohol fragments sleep and suppresses REM, even 1–2 drinks
    • Large meals finished 2–3 h before bed
    • Snoring or gasping? Ask about sleep apnea screening

    When to deload

    • Every 4–8 weeks of hard training, or when performance stalls 2+ sessions
    • Cut volume 40–60% and keep intensity moderate (RPE 5–6)
    • Joint aches, poor sleep, or dread before sessions are signs it's due
    • Lasts 5–7 days; you often come back stronger

    Overreaching vs. overtraining

    Functional
    Planned fatigue that resolves in days. Leads to gains.
    Non-functional
    Stagnation lasting weeks. Needs more rest and more food.
    Overtraining syndrome
    Months of lost performance, mood disturbance, illness. Rare, and usually tied to low energy availability (RED-S).

    Recovery tools, ranked

    Mobility minimum

    • Hip flexor stretch: 2 × 45 s each side
    • Thoracic rotations: 10 each side
    • Deep squat hold: 60 s accumulated
    • Dead hang: 3 × 20–30 s
    • Ankle knee-to-wall: 10 each side
    • 90/90 hip switches: 10 total

    Low energy availability (RED-S)

    Chronic under-fueling relative to training. Signs include lost periods, frequent illness, stress fractures, stalled progress and low mood. Energy availability under about 30 kcal per kg of lean mass per day is the danger zone.

    If this sounds familiar, see a sports-medicine doctor or registered dietitian.

    Breathing pacer

    1 to 5 minutes helps downshift
    Ready

    Slow breathing with longer exhales raises vagal tone and often lowers heart rate within minutes. The "physiological sigh" (two short inhales through the nose, one long exhale) is a quick reset. If breath holds make you dizzy, stop.

    HRV-guided training

    What it is
    Heart-rate variability is the beat-to-beat variation in your pulse. Higher usually means better recovery and parasympathetic activity.
    How to use it
    Measure the same way each morning (device overnight or a 1 to 5 minute reading), then compare to your own 7-day average against a 30-day baseline. Don't compare to other people.
    Green
    At or above baseline: train as planned or push.
    Amber
    5 to 15% below, or resting HR up 3+ bpm: keep intensity moderate.
    Red
    15%+ below for several days, with poor sleep or illness: deload, sleep, eat enough.
    Caveats

    Flexibility & stretching types

    Static
    Hold 30–60 s. Best after training or as its own session. Long holds (60 s+) right before maximal lifting slightly reduce strength.
    Dynamic
    Controlled swings and lunges through range. Best in warm-ups.
    PNF
    Contract the stretched muscle 5–10 s, relax, stretch deeper. Fastest range-of-motion gains.
    Ballistic
    Bouncing into end range. Only for sports that need it (martial arts, dance).
    Loaded stretching
    Strength training through full range (deep squats, RDLs, flys) builds flexibility as well as stretching does.

    Aim for 5+ minutes per muscle group a week to gain range. Stretching alone does little to prevent injury; warming up and building strength do more.

    Plyometrics

    • What: rapid stretch–shorten jumps and throws that build power and reactive strength.
    • Prerequisite: squat about 1.5× bodyweight, or land jumps quietly with stable knees.
    • Dose: 40–80 foot contacts per session (beginner) up to 120+ (advanced), 1–2 times a week, done fresh.
    • Progression: squat jumps → box jumps (focus on landing) → broad jumps → bounds → depth jumps.
    • Upper body: med-ball chest passes, slams, clap push-ups.
    • Rest 1–2 min between sets. Plyometrics are about quality, not conditioning.

    Mandatory poses

    Posing & presentation

    • Practice posing daily for 8–12 weeks out, building to 30–60 minutes. Holding poses is exhausting and also acts as extra cardio.
    • Rehearse transitions, not just the poses. Judges are watching you between poses too.
    • Flex from the feet up: legs locked, then vacuum the waist, then the upper body.
    • Learn the stomach vacuum: exhale fully and pull the navel toward the spine. It creates the classic V-taper.
    • Choose a routine (60–90 s free posing) and music that show off your strengths.
    • Film yourself under bright overhead lighting; stage lights flatten detail.
    • Competition tan: 2–3 coats builds contrast. Pale skin hides muscle definition under stage lights.
    • Relaxed pose isn't relaxed: you stay tight, just not in a full flex.

    Pregnancy

    • ACOG recommends 150 min/week of moderate activity for uncomplicated pregnancies. Continuing lifting is generally safe at moderate effort.
    • Use the "talk test" rather than heart-rate caps.
    • After the first trimester, limit long periods lying flat on the back. Avoid contact sports, fall-risk activities, and hot yoga.
    • Extra calories: about 0 in trimester 1, +340 in trimester 2, +450 in trimester 3.
    • Folate (400–800 µg) before and during pregnancy; iron, iodine, choline, DHA.
    • Stop and call your provider for bleeding, dizziness, chest pain, calf swelling, fluid leakage, or regular contractions.

    Postpartum

    • Walking and breathing / pelvic floor work can start within days if delivery was uncomplicated.
    • Most return to structured training after clearance (often 6+ weeks; longer after a C-section).
    • Diastasis recti (abdominal separation) is common. Build pressure management and core work progressively.
    • Leaking, heaviness or pain with running or jumping → see a pelvic floor physical therapist.
    • Breastfeeding burns about 400–500 kcal/day. Avoid aggressive deficits.

    Perimenopause & menopause

    • Falling estrogen speeds bone loss (up to ~10% in the 5 years around menopause) and shifts fat toward the belly.
    • Lift heavy: high-intensity resistance and impact training improved bone density in postmenopausal women (LIFTMOR trial).
    • Protein 1.2–1.6+ g/kg; calcium 1,200 mg; vitamin D.
    • Sleep disruption and hot flashes affect recovery. Hormone therapy is an option to discuss with a doctor.
    • Add sprint or plyometric work if your joints allow; it's good for muscle power and bone.

    Testosterone: natural levers

    • Sleep: one week at 5 h/night cut testosterone 10–15% in young men.
    • Body fat: obesity lowers testosterone; losing fat raises it.
    • Very low-fat diets (<20% of calories) can lower it modestly.
    • Extreme dieting and heavy endurance volume can suppress it.
    • Zinc and vitamin D help only if you're deficient.
    • Symptoms plus two low morning lab tests → see a doctor. Testosterone therapy suppresses fertility.

    Aging well (50+)

    • Muscle mass falls 3–8% per decade after 30, faster after 60. Power falls even faster.
    • Strength train 2–3× a week; include fast lifting (power) and balance work.
    • Protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg, 30–40 g per meal, because older muscle needs more protein to respond.
    • Longevity markers: grip strength, VO₂max, sitting down and rising from the floor without hands, 10 s single-leg stand.
    • Total daily energy use stays stable from about 20 to 60 and declines slowly after (Pontzer 2021). Weight gain in middle age is mostly from moving less.

    Kids & teens

    • 60 minutes of activity daily, with muscle and bone strengthening 3 days a week (WHO).
    • Supervised strength training is safe from about age 7–8 when children can follow instructions. It doesn't stunt growth.
    • Prioritize technique, variety of sports, and fun over max lifts.
    • No dieting for weight in growing kids without medical guidance. Focus on food quality and activity.
    • Teen athletes need lots of calories; under-fueling harms growth and bone.

    Mixed (~50/50)

    Henneman's size principle: motor units are recruited small to large. Any set taken close to failure eventually recruits fast fibers, which is why light-load sets still build muscle.

    Rep test: are you fast or slow?

    Find your 1RM (or estimate it on the Workouts tab), rest fully, then do as many clean reps as possible at 80% of it.

    Body types (somatotypes)

    C · Partly valid

    Somatotypes come from psychologist William Sheldon in the 1940s, who also linked body shape to personality. That idea was discredited. Body shape as a description (Heath-Carter rating) is still used in sports science, but it is a snapshot that changes with diet and training, not a fixed metabolic type.

    What the evidence actually says

    Real
    Frame size, limb length, and where you store fat are partly genetic. They shape which lifts and sports suit you.
    Real
    Insulin sensitivity varies, and some people do better on lower-carb diets. You find out from lab work and results, not body shape.
    Not real
    Body type doesn't fix a metabolic rate. Most "endomorphs" are people with more body fat right now; lose fat and gain muscle, and they become "mesomorphs."
    Not real
    "Ectomorphs can't build muscle." Hard-gainers usually eat less than they think. A consistent surplus works.
    Best use
    As a starting point for food preferences and training style. Then adjust from real data: weight trend, strength, energy.

    Blood type and diet

    D · Not supported

    Peter D'Adamo's Eat Right 4 Your Type (1996) claims ABO blood type reflects ancestral diets and that lectins in "wrong" foods react with your blood. A 2013 systematic review found no studies supporting it. A 2014 study of 1,455 people found that the "type" diets did improve some health markers, but the improvements had no link to the person's actual blood type. Everyone benefited from eating more whole foods.

    Where blood type does matter

    • Transfusions and organ transplants (critical)
    • Type O: slightly lower heart-disease and clot risk, higher risk of stomach ulcers (H. pylori)
    • Non-O types: somewhat higher risk of venous blood clots and heart disease
    • Type A: slightly higher stomach cancer risk in some studies
    • Rh factor matters in pregnancy (Rh-negative mothers get anti-D treatment)

    None of these associations are changed by the blood type diet.

    Myth-busting, habits & an A–Z glossary

    The gym is full of folklore. Here are the most common myths and what research says, how to build habits that last, and a glossary of the terms used across this dashboard and in gyms.

    Myths vs. evidence

    Building habits that stick

    Start tiny
    A 10-minute workout you always do beats a 90-minute plan you skip. Grow it once it's automatic (habits take about 2–8 months; the median in one study was 66 days).
    Implementation intentions
    "I will train at 6 pm Monday, Wednesday, Friday at [gym]." Specific when-and-where plans double follow-through in studies.
    Habit stacking
    Attach a new habit to an existing one: "After my morning coffee, I'll do 10 push-ups."
    Environment design
    Gym bag by the door, fruit on the counter, snacks out of sight. Reduce friction for good habits, add it to bad ones.
    Never miss twice
    Missing once is life. Missing twice starts a new habit. Get back the next day.
    Identity
    "I'm someone who trains" outlasts "I'm trying to lose weight." Every workout is a vote for that identity.
    Track the process
    Log workouts, steps and protein, not just weight. You control actions; outcomes follow.
    80/20
    Hitting targets 80–90% of the time gets most of the results. Perfectionism leads to all-or-nothing binges.
    Social support
    Training partners, coaches and groups significantly improve adherence.

    When motivation fails

    SituationWhat to do
    No timeMinimum session: 3 compound lifts × 2–3 hard sets in 25 min. Or a 20-min EMOM.
    TravelHotel gym or bodyweight circuit; walk 10k steps; protein at every meal.
    PlateauCheck sleep, calories and protein first. Then change rep ranges, add a set, or deload.
    Weekend overeatingPlan one flexible meal rather than a "cheat day." Keep protein and steps the same.
    Scale won't moveUse the 7-day average over 3+ weeks; also measure your waist. Water retention can mask fat loss for 1–2 weeks.
    Gym anxietyGo at off-peak hours, follow a written plan, start with machines, wear headphones. Everyone's focused on themselves.
    InjuryTrain around it: everything that doesn't hurt. Movement speeds recovery for most strains.
    BurnoutA deload week, a new goal (a race, a meet, a skill), or a new training style.

    If food or exercise thoughts become obsessive or distressing, or involve purging, compensatory exercise or extreme restriction, talk to a professional. In the US, the ANAD helpline is 1-888-375-7767.
